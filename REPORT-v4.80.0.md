## What driving the machine turned up

I ran a beat end to end and watched the counters rather than reading the code. Three
things, all measured:

**1. The room is a fixed seven turns and nothing you clicked could shorten it.** The way
out of the family's own time appears on turn seven, full stop. There *has* always been a
way to open it early — but it could only ever arrive as a field on an AI reply. In a
written night, which never contacts the AI, **it did not exist.**

Combined with the other number — **30 of the 33 rooms hold fewer than seven things to talk
about, most of them three** — the normal shape of family time was three real questions and
then four turns of the two dead stalls, waiting on a clock.

**2. Budget and pressure do nothing in the written game.** Every chapter carries a
"budget" and a list of pressure lines. I drove a beat five turns past its budget and
nothing fired. They're instructions for the AI. Nothing in the written game reads them.

**3. Which made the turn counter on the board a lie.** Those pips were counting toward the
budget, and turning **red** past it — on a chapter behaving exactly as designed. The board
was promising a deadline the written game does not keep.

## What's fixed

**The player gets the bell.** A clickable line can now say *"this one is reaching for the
road"*. It opens the way out immediately instead of at turn seven — and it only **opens**
it. You can still stay and talk. It's the same mechanism the AI could always use; the
written game had just never been handed the key.

*"Move. The marsh road is hours out and the rain is winning."* was never a question, and it
no longer pretends to be one.

**Proved by contrast, which is the only way it means anything:**

> Same chapter, same deck, one line apart — the way out opens on **turn 1** when the
> courier reaches for the road, and on **turn 7** when he asks another question.

**The pips now count the floor** — the turns before the door stops being held, which is the
only number in a written night that changes what you can do. They fill up and stop. No
more red. The AI mode keeps the budget, where going over it genuinely means something.

# Now the big one: 120 new scenes

You chose to keep the seven turns and fill the rooms. That's 30 rooms going from three
cards to seven — **about 120 new scenes**, and it makes the family's own time the largest
single thing in the game. It's the right call for what this game is, and it's a lot of
writing, so I want to do it the way we've been working: I pitch the subjects, you rule,
I write.

**Here's the first room, as the pattern.** `a1-turn` — the night the job dies and the
family starts. It currently has three: what happens tomorrow, what Nine makes of having
been asked, and the food and the noise. My four to add:

| # | subject | who answers | why it belongs in this room |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **who else has sat at this table and gone** | Vic | The fifth plate was laid for a rock tonight. It has been laid for other people. He is bad at this and answers it anyway. |
| 2 | **whether Pia is going home, and where that is** | Pia | She has been "just here" all night. Nobody has asked her where she sleeps, and she has been waiting for somebody to. |
| 3 | **what the shard costs to keep running** | Vic | A practical question with a floor under it — power, heat, wear. It is the first time anyone treats Nine as something with needs rather than a decision. |
| 4 | **the rain on the roof, and the gutter again** | Pia | The evening's own joke coming back one more time before bed. The room's last card should be small. |

That last one is deliberate: **every room's seventh card should be the smallest thing in
it.** If a room has to run seven turns, the way out should arrive on a scene that is
already winding down, so leaving feels like the night ending rather than a door appearing.

If that shape works for you, I'll write these four, show you the room played end to end,
and then bring you the next beat's four.
